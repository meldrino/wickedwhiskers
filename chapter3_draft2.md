# Chapter 3 — Into Town

Wicked Whiskers departed the farm at precisely the kind of hour when fences still yawn and cockerels consult their watches. He crossed the meadow with the dignity of a cat who knows his tail is on trend and his whiskers are likely to be in the next village paper. The village smelled of fresh bread, old gossip, and the very faint possibility of trouble — which, to a cat, smells a lot like dinner.

He had two fish in his rucksack, a belly full of milk, and absolutely no idea where he was going. This was, by his standards, a very good start to the day.

The lane curved gently downhill, away from the farm and toward a cluster of buildings that Whiskers could only describe as "a town, but with fewer pigeons and more opinions." There was a bakery, a butcher, a fishmonger (which advertised "Fresh Catch — No Whisk-ey Business"), a pub called The Salty Cod, and a post office run by a woman who looked like she'd rather be anywhere else — preferably underwater. Pigeons strutted around the square like they owned the place. A dog slept in a doorway. A human carried a bag of shopping and didn't notice Whiskers at all, which was exactly how Whiskers preferred it.

He trotted down the high street, tail high, fish securely fastened in his rucksack, feeling rather pleased with himself. He had survived a farm, outsmarted a padlock, caught three animals, and escaped a farmer's wife armed with nothing but a boot and a loud voice. A village should be a doddle.

That was when the seagull arrived.

It materialized like a feathered ninja, descending from the clouds with all the subtlety of a pigeon in a china shop — which, come to think of it, is exactly what seagulls are: pigeons with anger management issues. It was enormous. It was white. It had a beak like a can opener and the moral compass of a pickpocket.

Whiskers heard the wingbeats a fraction too late. He looked up just in time to see the seagull swoop down, grab one of the fish from his open rucksack with the casual expertise of a professional thief, and flap away with it dangling from its beak like a wet trophy.

"Oi!" shouted Whiskers.

The seagull didn't even look back. It just kept flying, the fish swinging beneath it like a pendulum of betrayal.

"You bird-brained feather duster!" screamed Whiskers. "That's fin-acceptable! I am going to get that fish back if it's the last thing I — no, wait, I have nine lives. It's the last thing I do on this particular life, but you get the idea!"

The seagull landed on a lamppost, twenty feet up, and began to eat the fish with the slow, deliberate smugness of a creature that knew it was out of reach. It was, quite literally, the highest crime in the village — larceny at altitude.

Whiskers stared up at it. The seagull stared down at him. It flicked a fish bone in his direction. It landed on his head.

"Oh, it is ON," said Whiskers.

He looked around for something to help. A drainpipe — too slippery. A bin — too full. A bicycle — too confusing. Then he spotted it: a clothesline strung between two buildings, hung with tea towels and sheets, waving gently in the breeze.

Whiskers had an idea. It was not, by any reasonable standard, a good idea. But Wicked Whiskers had never let that stop him before.

He ran at the wall, leapt onto a window ledge, scrambled up a drainpipe (it was marginally less slippery than it looked), and launched himself onto the clothesline. The line snapped taut, then sagged dramatically. He grabbed a tea towel — a lovely floral one, the kind your grandmother would use — and swung like a furry pendulum, knocking a potted geranium off a windowsill. It exploded below in a shower of soil and indignation. He found himself hanging upside down directly above the lamppost, looking absolutely ridiculous.

The seagull looked up. Its expression changed from smug to concerned in approximately half a second.

"Surprise," said Whiskers, dangling upside down with a tea towel wrapped around one leg and a geranium leaf stuck to his nose. "I'm so sorry to drop in unannounced."

He let go.

He landed squarely on the seagull. The bird squawked, flapped, and dropped the fish. Whiskers caught it in mid-air — one paw, clean catch, like a furry outfielder — and rolled off the lamppost, hit the pavement, bounced, and came to rest in a flowerbed.

The seagull flew away, screaming insults in what Whiskers assumed was seagull for "I will be filing a complaint."

Whiskers lay in the flowerbed for a moment, fish clutched to his chest, covered in petals and the quiet satisfaction of a job well done. Then he stood up, shook himself off, and tucked the fish back into his rucksack.

"Right," he said. "Now I need breakfast. Proper breakfast. The kind humans eat."

The butcher's shop was right there. A string of sausages hung in the window, glistening like jewels. A joint of chicken sat on the counter, pink and promising. The butcher was inside, serving a customer, his back turned.

Whiskers slipped through the open door. The chicken gleamed on the counter like an edible crown jewel. It smelled like the best thing he'd ever smelled — assuming "best thing" meant "reason to commit grand theft poultry." Which, it did.

He leapt onto the counter, grabbed the chicken in his teeth, and —

"HEY!" shouted the butcher.

Whiskers bolted. He shot out of the shop like a furry cannonball, chicken in mouth, butcher behind him, customer screaming, a display of chops flying through the air like meaty confetti. He dodged a pram, leapt over a dog, slid under a bench, and ricocheted off a lamppost. The butcher gave up after half a block, bent double, wheezing, shouting about "that blasted cat" and declaring it was officially a "four-alarm feline felony." A customer stuck their head out a shop door and asked what was happening. "Poultry larceny!" gasped the butcher. "By air mail!"

Whiskers hid behind a wheelie bin, panting, chicken still clamped in his jaws. He was alive. He was victorious. He was a criminal.

He ate the chicken behind the bin. It was delicious. It was worth every chased step.

Then a woman found him.

She was not angry. She was not cross. She was the kind of woman who kept biscuits and a whole tin of tuna in her handbag, a walking advertisement for "cat lady" before it was even breakfast time. She had names for all the local cats — even the ones that actively avoided her. She knelt down, saw Whiskers sitting there with chicken grease on his whiskers and a flower petal on his ear, and her face melted like butter on a warm scone.

"Oh, you poor thing," she cooed. "You look half starved. Here, have a bit of this."

She produced a tin of tuna from her handbag and opened it on the spot. Whiskers ate it without dignity. Tuna from a tin was not the same as a fresh fish, but it was food, and food was food, and Wicked Whiskers was not too proud to eat tuna from a kind stranger's handbag on the side of the road.

"You're a handsome one," the woman said, scratching him behind the ears. "Very wicked looking. I shall call you Wicked."

"I already have a name, thank you," thought Whiskers, though he purred anyway because the scratching was very good.

He thanked the woman (with a meow, which she interpreted as gratitude), wiped the tuna from his whiskers, and padded back into the square. The sun was warm. The pigeons had moved on. The seagull was nowhere to be seen. And Whiskers had two fish, a stomach full of chicken and tuna, and the distinct feeling that this village was going to be very interesting indeed.

He looked around at the shops, the people, the dogs, the chaos. He looked at the pub, the post office, the bakery with its window full of bread. He looked at the lane that led out of the village and toward whatever came next.

"Right," he said to himself. "I think I'll stay a while. This village is chaos. It's perfect. It's basically my autobiography waiting to happen."

He caught the smell of fish coming from The Salty Cod and grinned wickedly. "Literally, I can already smell it."
