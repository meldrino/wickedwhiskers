# Chapter 3 — Into Town

Wicked Whiskers departed the farm at precisely the kind of hour when fences still yawn, cockerels check their watches, and farm boundaries are too sleepy to file official complaints. He crossed the meadow with the dignity of a cat who knows his tail is on trend and his whiskers are likely to be in the next village paper. The village smelled of fresh bread, old gossip, and the very faint possibility of trouble — which, to a cat, smelled a lot like dinner — and dinner, for Whiskers, smelled dangerously like destiny.

He had two fish in his rucksack, a belly full of milk, and absolutely no idea where he was going. This was, by his standards, a very good start to the day.

The lane curved gently downhill, away from the farm and toward a cluster of buildings that Whiskers could only describe as "a town, but with fewer pigeons and significantly more attitude." The high street featured a bakery, a butcher, a fishmonger with a punny sign, and a pub called The Salty Cod. The post office was run by a woman who looked like she'd rather be anywhere else — preferably underwater. Pigeons strutted around the square like they owned the place. A dog slept in a doorway. A human carried a bag of shopping and didn't notice Whiskers at all, which was exactly how Whiskers preferred it.

He trotted down the high street, tail high, fish securely fastened in his rucksack, feeling rather pleased with himself. He had survived a farm, outsmarted a padlock, caught three animals, and escaped a farmer's wife armed with nothing but a boot and a loud voice. A village should be a doddle.

He was busy admiring his own reflection in a shop window when the seagull arrived.

It materialized like a feathered ninja, descending from the clouds with all the subtlety of a pigeon in a china shop — which, come to think of it, is exactly what seagulls are: pigeons with anger management issues and a stolen credit card. It was enormous. It was white. It had a beak like a can opener and the moral compass of a pickpocket.

Whiskers heard the wingbeats a fraction too late. He looked up just in time to see the seagull swoop down, grab one of the fish from his open rucksack with the casual expertise of a professional thief, and flap away with it dangling from its beak like a wet trophy.

"Oi!" shouted Whiskers.

The seagull didn't even look back. It just kept flying, the fish swinging beneath it like a pendulum of betrayal.

"You bird-brained feather duster!" screamed Whiskers. "That's fin-acceptable! I'm getting that fish back if it's the last thing I do. Well, the last thing I do in this life, anyway — but you get the idea!"

The seagull landed on a lamppost, twenty feet up, and began to eat the fish with the slow, deliberate smugness of a creature that knew it was out of reach. High-altitude highway robbery.

Whiskers stared up at it. The seagull stared down at him. It flicked a fish bone in his direction with the precision of a tiny assassin. It landed on his head, slid down his nose, and wedged itself perfectly between his ears like a tiny, insulting crown.

"Oh, it is ON," said Whiskers.

He looked around for something to help. A drainpipe — too slippery. A bin — too full. A bicycle — too confusing. Then he spotted it: a clothesline strung between two buildings, hung with tea towels and sheets, waving gently in the breeze.

Whiskers had an idea. It was not, by any reasonable standard, a good idea. But Wicked Whiskers had never let that stop him before.

He ran at the wall, leapt onto a window ledge, scrambled up a drainpipe (it was marginally less slippery than it looked), and launched himself onto the clothesline. The line snapped taut, then sagged dramatically. He grabbed a tea towel — a lovely floral one that screamed "I sold jam at the village fair in 1987" — and swung like a furry pendulum, knocking a potted geranium off a windowsill. It exploded below in a shower of soil and indignation. He found himself hanging upside down directly above the lamppost, looking absolutely ridiculous.

The seagull looked up. Its expression changed from smug to "oh-this-is-actually-going-to-hurt" in approximately half a second, which was still half a second too late.

"Surprise," said Whiskers, dangling upside down with a tea towel wrapped around one leg, a geranium leaf stuck to his nose, and the absolute conviction that he'd nailed this. "I'm so sorry to drop in unannounced."

He let go.

He landed squarely on the seagull. The bird squawked, flapped, and dropped the fish. Whiskers caught it in mid-air — one paw, clean catch, like a furry outfielder — and rolled off the lamppost, hit the pavement, bounced, and came to rest in a flowerbed.

The seagull flew away, screaming insults in what Whiskers assumed was seagull for "I will be filing a formal complaint."

Whiskers lay in the flowerbed for a moment, fish clutched to his chest, covered in petals and the quiet satisfaction of a job well done. Then he stood up, shook himself off, and tucked the fish back into his rucksack. Two fish. Right where they belonged. The seagull had eaten nothing but air and regret.

"Right," he said. "Now I need breakfast. Proper breakfast. The kind humans eat."

The butcher's shop was right there. A string of sausages hung in the window, glistening like jewels. A joint of chicken sat on the counter, pink and promising. The butcher was inside, serving a customer, his back turned.

Whiskers slipped through the open door. The chicken gleamed on the counter like an edible crown jewel. It smelled like the best thing he'd ever smelled — assuming "best thing" meant "reason to commit grand theft poultry." Which, it did.

He leapt onto the counter, grabbed the chicken in his teeth —

"HEY!" shouted the butcher.

Whiskers bolted. He shot out of the shop like a furry cannonball, chicken in mouth. Behind him, the butcher roared, the customer shrieked, and a rack of pork chops fell like meaty confetti. He dodged a pram, leapt over a dog, slid under a bench, and ricocheted off a lamppost. The butcher gave up after half a block, wheezing about "that blasted cat" and a "four-alarm feline felony." When a bystander asked what had happened, the butcher gasped, "Poultry larceny! By air mail!"

Whiskers hid behind a wheelie bin, panting, chicken still clamped in his jaws. He was alive. He was victorious. He was a criminal.

He ate the chicken behind the bin. It was delicious. It was worth every frantic step. He licked his whiskers, burped quietly, and thought about what to do next. He still had two fish in his rucksack — one for eating, one for trading. Dumbleclaw had said gold would be useful, and a fish was worth its weight in gold to the right buyer.

That was when a woman found him.

She was not angry. She was not cross. She was the sort of woman who treated her handbag like a feline vending machine — always stocked, always ready, emergency tuna budgeted in at all times. She had names for all the local cats (even the ones that actively avoided her) and the superpower of seeing any filthy cat and thinking "what a precious angel." She knelt down, saw Whiskers sitting there with chicken grease on his whiskers and a flower petal on his ear, and her face melted like butter on a warm scone left out in July.

"Oh, you poor thing," she cooed. "You look half starved. Here, have a bit of this."

She produced a tin of tuna from her handbag and opened it on the spot. Whiskers ate it without dignity. He knew exactly what he was doing — the plaintive eyes, the tucked-in paws, the slight head tilt. He was a professional. Tuna from a tin was not the same as a fresh fish, but it was food — free food at that — and Wicked Whiskers was far too proud to turn it down.

"You're a handsome one," the woman said, scratching him behind the ears. "Very wicked looking. I shall call you Wicked."

"I have a reputation, actually," Whiskers thought, though he purred anyway because the scratching was very good.

He thanked the woman (with a meow, which she interpreted as gratitude), wiped the tuna from his whiskers, and padded back into the square. The sun was warm. The pigeons had moved on. The seagull was nowhere to be seen. And Whiskers had two fish, a belly full of chicken and tuna, and the distinct feeling that this village was going to be very interesting indeed.

He looked at the pub, the post office, and the lane leading to whatever came next. "I think I'll stay," he purred to the empty square. "This village is chaotic. It's perfect. It's basically my autobiography waiting to happen."

He caught the scent of fish fry drifting from The Salty Cod and grinned. "And I've only just started."
